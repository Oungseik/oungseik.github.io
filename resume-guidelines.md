# Resume Guidelines

A professional, high-quality resume should be targeted, evidence-based, and easy to scan—not heavily decorated. For most industry applications, a clean, single-column resume with reverse-chronological experience is the safest starting point.

These guidelines draw on Harvard, MIT, the University of Pennsylvania, and Greenhouse documentation. They primarily follow **US-style industry resume conventions**. Country-specific practices and employer instructions take priority. Academic CVs have different requirements.

## 1. What Makes a Resume High Quality?

Four things matter most:

- **Relevance:** Highlight qualifications that match the job rather than documenting everything you have ever done.
- **Evidence:** Describe accomplishments and contributions, not just assigned responsibilities.
- **Readability:** Make your role, employers, skills, and strongest achievements easy to locate.
- **Accuracy:** Use truthful claims, consistent dates, working links, and error-free writing.

Harvard emphasizes specific, active, fact-based language. MIT recommends selecting experiences using the job description and showing accomplishments rather than reproducing job duties.

Sources: [Harvard’s resume guide][harvard] and [MIT’s resume guidance][mit-resumes].

## 2. What Information Should Be Included?

### Core Information

| Component | What to include |
| --- | --- |
| **Name and contact details** | Name, professional email, phone number, and optionally city/country. Add LinkedIn, a portfolio, or GitHub when relevant. A full street address is normally unnecessary. |
| **Relevant experience** | Job title, organization, location or remote status, employment dates, and accomplishment-focused bullets. Internships, freelance work, research, volunteering, and leadership can also count. |
| **Relevant skills** | Specific tools, technologies, methods, languages, or other demonstrable capabilities that matter to the role. |
| **Education or training** | Institution, degree or qualification, subject, and completion or expected completion date. Include relevant training where appropriate. |
| **Required professional credentials** | Licenses or certifications when they establish eligibility for the position. Include issuer and validity details where relevant. |

### Optional Sections

Include these only when they strengthen the application:

- **Professional summary:** Useful for experienced candidates or career changers. Keep it to two or three concise lines explaining your specialization and relevant strengths. It is not mandatory.
- **Projects:** Particularly useful when they demonstrate abilities that your employment history does not yet show. Explain your contribution, methods, and result; include a useful link.
- **Awards, leadership, or volunteering:** Include relevant evidence of achievement or responsibility.
- **Publications and presentations:** Most useful for research-oriented roles.
- **GPA and coursework:** Mainly useful for students and recent graduates, when strong, relevant, or requested.

Do not rely on a list of soft-skill claims. Demonstrate communication, teamwork, or leadership through experience bullets instead of simply writing “excellent communicator” or “team player.”

Source: [University of Pennsylvania’s resume guidance][penn].

## 3. What Order Should the Sections Follow?

There is no universal section order. **Put the strongest, most relevant evidence first.** These are practical starting points:

| Applicant situation | Recommended order |
| --- | --- |
| **Experienced professional** | Contact details → Optional summary → Professional experience → Skills → Education → Relevant additional sections |
| **Student or recent graduate** | Contact details → Education → Relevant experience and/or projects → Skills → Relevant leadership, awards, or activities |
| **Career changer** | Contact details → Targeted summary → Relevant skills → Relevant experience/projects → Additional employment → Education or training |

Adjustments that make sense:

- For a technical position, a short technical-skills section can move above experience.
- If a license or certification is essential to eligibility, make it prominent near the top.
- If projects are your strongest evidence, put them before less relevant employment.
- Keep a clear employment history with dates, even when emphasizing transferable skills.

Within dated sections such as **Experience** and **Education**, list entries **most recent first**.

Sources: [Harvard—section importance and reverse chronology][harvard] and [Penn—layout and education placement][penn].

## 4. How Should the Layout Look?

Aim for restrained typography, clear hierarchy, and enough whitespace to distinguish sections.

These are recommended starting settings, not universal requirements:

| Layout element | Recommended setting |
| --- | --- |
| **Page length** | Usually one page for students and early-career applicants; one or two pages for experienced professionals when the material is relevant |
| **Columns** | One column for the application version |
| **Body text** | 11–12 pt; avoid shrinking below 10 pt |
| **Section headings** | Approximately 12–14 pt, bold |
| **Name** | Approximately 20–24 pt |
| **Margins** | 0.5–1 inch; start around 0.75 inch |
| **Typeface** | One readable font, such as Arial, Calibri, or Helvetica |
| **Alignment** | Left-aligned body text; consistent date placement |
| **Color** | Dark, high-contrast text; an optional restrained accent |
| **Descriptions** | Short bullets rather than dense paragraphs |

MIT explicitly recommends **10–12 pt text, 0.5–1 inch margins, consistent formatting, and sufficient whitespace**. The larger name and heading sizes above are practical design recommendations rather than requirements from MIT.

Additional layout decisions:

- Give more space to recent, relevant roles and less to older or unrelated ones.
- Use bold selectively for headings, roles, or employers—not entire paragraphs.
- Keep date formatting consistent, for example `Jan 2024–Present`.
- Avoid splitting a role heading from its bullets across pages.
- Remove low-value content before reducing readability to fit a page.

Source: [MIT’s resume checklist][mit-checklist].

## 5. How Should Experience Bullets Be Written?

Use this structure:

> **Action + what you did + how you did it + result or meaningful scope**

**Weak:**

> Responsible for preparing reports.

**Stronger—hypothetical example:**

> Automated weekly sales reports using Excel, saving two hours of preparation time per week.

Useful evidence can include:

- Time saved or costs reduced.
- Customers, users, or stakeholders served.
- Team size, project scope, or budget managed.
- Improvements in reliability, quality, or delivery.
- A concrete deliverable or decision your work enabled.

**Not every bullet needs a number.** When metrics are unavailable, describe the contribution and outcome precisely. Never invent percentages or exaggerate your ownership.

Source: [MIT—specific descriptions and factual accomplishments][mit-resumes].

## 6. What Should Be Avoided, and How Should the Resume Be Submitted?

### Avoid Unnecessary Content

For a standard US-style resume, normally omit:

- A photograph, age, date of birth, marital status, or religion.
- References or “References available upon request.”
- Generic objectives such as “Seeking a challenging opportunity.”
- Skill-rating bars, stars, and unsupported proficiency percentages.
- Long lists of unrelated tools, courses, or certificates.

Sources: [Harvard’s resume guide][harvard] and [MIT’s resume guidance][mit-resumes].

### Make the Application Version Parser-Friendly

An **applicant tracking system (ATS)** may extract information from the uploaded document. Greenhouse documents parsing problems with columns, graphics, complex tables, text boxes, and contact information placed in document headers or footers.

Therefore:

- Use recognizable headings such as **Experience, Education, Skills, and Projects**.
- Keep your name and contact details in the **main document body**.
- Avoid using tables or text boxes for core resume information.
- Submit a **text-based PDF** when accepted, or DOCX if requested; follow the employer’s instructions.
- Check any fields automatically populated by the application system.
- Copy the document’s text into a plain-text editor as a quick reading-order check. This is useful, but it does **not guarantee** ATS compatibility.

A parsing problem is **not automatically a rejection**: Greenhouse states that an unparsed resume can remain attached to the candidate record and require manual data entry.

Source: [Greenhouse’s official resume-parsing documentation][greenhouse].
